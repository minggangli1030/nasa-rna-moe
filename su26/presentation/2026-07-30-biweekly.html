Stage 2: Diagnosis

The instability exposed a training-design problem, not proof that sharing is impossible

Issue

No stable helpful rule yet

Helpful organ-to-organ effects changed across retraining, while two harmful relationships repeated.

Evidence: 0 of 8 helpful candidates and 2 of 8 harmful candidates passed the nine-run stability test.

Mistake

The answer key moved

The shared model saw a different hidden-gene pattern and was graded against a different target each round—like using a rewritten answer key.

Consequence: the model compressed toward the few signals that stayed obvious, and one organ crossed the safety limit.

Correction

Make the target fixed—and sharing optional

Use one canonical masked view and one fixed target per sample. Give each organ a gate initialized to decline sharing.

Grade the right outcome: stable, useful, no-harm share/decline decisions—not representation rank alone.

What remains valid: organ specialists are the benchmark, and “private by default” is the safe policy until a reproducible sharing gate earns the right to open.

Future work: From specialists to pretraining design

Discover the best division of labor for biological models

The larger goal is to learn how training structure—domain boundaries, routing, and selective sharing—can produce models that are more accurate, robust, and efficient.

Immediate test

Can specialists share safely?

Use one fixed answer key and let every organ decline shared information.

Continue only if: the benefit repeats and no organ is harmed.

Representation discovery

Organ is only the first split

Compare organ, tissue site, biological programs, and data-quality contexts to find stronger domain boundaries.

Question: which differences require separate experts, and which can share?

Pretraining payoff

Turn the rule into architecture

Use the discovered structure to choose training data, organize specialists, route samples, and prevent negative transfer.

End product: a model that knows when to specialize, share, or refuse.

Hospital analogy: determine how to divide the departments, distribute the workload, share useful notes, and keep incompatible specialists separate—so the whole hospital runs better.
