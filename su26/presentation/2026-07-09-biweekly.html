Our work, Last semester

Zero-shot OSDR evaluation — testing whether pretraining actually transferred

The team had four pretrained variants. My question: do any of them actually generalize to real spaceflight data?

Task: mask a random subset of genes in each sample, then ask the model to predict the missing values from the rest of the sequence. The number reported is how accurately it reconstructs them — Pearson correlation between predicted and true expression at the masked positions.

Zero-shot OSDR reconstruction (Pearson correlation), by pretraining variant — measured with the evaluation pipeline I built.

Note: OSDR here is 100% mouse spaceflight/control data — that's why the ranking tracks species match: mouse (0.78) highest, mixed (0.76) close behind since it saw mouse data too, human (0.69) lowest since it never saw mouse expression at all. Two takeaways: (1) the model is learning real, species-specific biology, not noise — species-matched pretraining measurably wins; (2) scale still matters within a species — human 5k → 20k nearly closes the gap (0.69 → 0.81).

These numbers look reasonable in isolation. They aren't — next slide.

Gene-mean collapse — the model found a shortcut, not meaningful learning

Same masked-gene-reconstruction task as before — but here we check the accuracy number against a trivial baseline instead of reading it in isolation.

Every variant sits at or below the trivial "predict the average" baseline — real learned signal is much weaker than the raw correlation suggests.

  • Built check_alignment.py to compare reconstruction against that trivial gene-mean baseline directly.
  • Scaling human-only 5k → 20k closed most of the gap (0.69 → 0.81). Architecture tweaks alone, without more data, moved the needle far less — scale was the dominant lever.
04
Our work, Last semester

The team's first MoE gate

Why MoE: the zero-shot transfer results already show a single generalist model can learn some cross-species biology. The open question is whether a set of specialized experts — each deeply tuned to one species — reconstructs unseen genes even better than one generalist when combined, rather than diluting that shared signal. If that holds for species, the same principle should extend to other biological axes: organ, tissue, stressor type.

Frozen experts, one gate — the gate learns per-sample routing weights, outputs are combined by weighted sum.

Also caught a bug with check_moe_gene_counts.py: each expert had trained on its own gene ordering, so the gate's inputs weren't even comparable across experts.

Our work, Last semester

The diagnostic that flagged the gate as premature

Before spending compute training the gate: if you always picked the best expert per sample, how much would you gain over the single best expert overall?

Headroom (built with analyze_moe_headroom.py) is near-zero everywhere except the mixed split — and that gap turned out to be a species-ID artifact, not real routing signal.

MoE was limited by expert quality, not routing — the exact question this SPARC project picks up.

This project · SPARC continuation

Research question

"Can Mixture-of-Experts architectures improve predictive performance and biological interpretability for spaceflight transcriptomic classification — once the expert-quality problems that invalidated the first attempt are actually fixed?"

  • Objective 1 — implement and refine MoE architectures, on top of experts that don't collapse and do share a vocabulary.
  • Objective 2 — evaluate expert specialization behavior across species, tissue, and stressor.
  • Objective 3 — benchmark against existing baselines and assess biological interpretability, not just correlation numbers.

  • Built compute_shared_canonical.py — a canonical gene list (protein-coding orthologs ∩ human exon lengths ∩ mouse exon lengths) so human, mouse, and mixed experts all share identical column order. Directly fixes the vocab-mismatch bug from slide 5.

Related work

Other MoE-for-genomics work routes on OOD risk or modality, not on species identity

MoEFold2D: robust RNA secondary structure prediction with a mixture of DL and physics-based experts

Xiangyun Qiu

Trains a cluster-held-out ensemble of DL models — each one an "expert" on all but one structural cluster. For a new sequence, if the DL models agree it's in-distribution → average their predictions. If they strongly disagree, that's an OOD signal → fall back to a physics-based model instead. The routing signal is ensemble disagreement, detected at inference with no access to training data — not a learned gate.

CodonMoE: DNA Language Models for mRNA Analyses

Du, Liang, Li, Kingsford

A lightweight codon-level MoE adapter that turns a frozen DNA language model into an RNA analyzer with no RNA-specific pretraining — a universal approximator at the codon level. HyenaDNA+CodonMoE hits SOTA on 4 RNA tasks (stability, expression, regulation) with 80% fewer parameters than specialized RNA models. The gain comes from bridging two modalities (DNA → RNA) through one shared backbone, not from routing between independently-trained full models.

What this suggests: my ARCHS4-trained → OSDR-spaceflight zero-shot eval is structurally an ID/OOD generalization problem, the same shape MoEFold2D targets — but I've been routing on species identity, which is usually obvious and doesn't need a gate. MoEFold2D's actual signal is ensemble disagreement: agreement → trust the DL average; disagreement → fall back to something more conservative. Worth trying directly: use disagreement across my three experts as a per-sample OOD flag on spaceflight data, rather than trying to pick "the right species." Separately, CodonMoE argues for one shared backbone + lightweight adapters over three independently-trained full models — likely why routing among my experts has so little headroom to find.

Next steps

What happens after this sprint

  • The v1 vocab-bug fix resolved a real confound — v1's apparent MoE benefit doesn't hold up once the comparison is valid. That's the actual deliverable of this sprint: a trustworthy negative result instead of an inflated positive one.
  • Chasing a sharper lead — a static 30/70 human/mouse blend beat the best single expert by 4-5x the oracle gap. Next: confirm on a held-out split (not grid-search-fit on the eval set), then decide if it generalizes.
  • Fixing the eval domain confound — this OSDR set is 100% mouse spaceflight/control, so mouse_5k_v2 wins almost everywhere by construction. Need samples where the "right" expert isn't obvious in advance to actually test cross-species routing.
  • Parallel path: expert scale/quality (e.g. a v2 human_20k) — consistent with the "scale is the real lever" finding from slide 4, independent of whether MoE routing pans out.
  • Dedicated infrastructure means the next iteration doesn't wait on a shared queue — faster turnaround for the rest of the summer.
