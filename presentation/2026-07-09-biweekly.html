01 · ~20s
Background · Why this matters

Longer space missions need better biology models

  • Missions are getting longer, which means more cumulative exposure to microgravity, ionizing radiation, confinement, and disrupted circadian cycles — all of which leave molecular fingerprints in gene expression.
  • There's no existing foundation model built specifically for interpreting spaceflight transcriptomic experiments.
  • Spaceflight experiments are expensive and slow to run, so actual human spaceflight data is rare and tightly regulated — models need to transfer from abundant ground data to a scarce target domain, not just fit it directly.
02 · ~40s
Background · Data and last year's team effort

A team project I was part of set up the pipeline

Berkeley CDSS Data Discovery, mentored by Dr. Walter Alvarado (NASA Ames) — myself, Abraham Guan, Brian Zhou, Karen Meng, and Ishanth Hombaiah.

Two repositories

ARCHS4: ~1M human + ~1M mouse bulk RNA-seq samples, ground-based and abundant — used to pretrain.
NASA OSDR/GeneLab: ~2,860 actual spaceflight samples, mostly mouse — scarce, and the real target domain.

The team's approach

Pretrain a SLiMPerformer-based transformer (genes-as-tokens, same idea as scBERT/Geneformer) via masked-expression reconstruction on ARCHS4, then evaluate zero-shot on OSDR. Brian led the pretraining backbone; I'll get to my part next.

Bulk RNA-seq specifically because it's cheaper than single-cell, gives fuller per-sample context, and matches how NASA actually samples.

My work, last semester

I built the zero-shot OSDR evaluation — nobody had actually tested whether pretraining transferred

The team had four pretrained variants. My question: do any of them actually generalize to real spaceflight data, or just to more ARCHS4?

  • Built the evaluation pipeline that downloads real OSDR spaceflight counts, aligns them to the pretraining gene space, and scores masked-reconstruction quality (Pearson correlation) — this became the team's zero-shot OSDR numbers.
  • Ran it across all four variants the team had trained: mouse_5k, mixed_5k, human_5k, human_20k.

05 · ~50s
My work, last semester

I diagnosed why: the model was just predicting the gene mean

  • Built a diagnostic (check_alignment.py) that compares each model's reconstruction against the trivial baseline of "predict every gene's average expression, ignore the input."
  • Result: at 5k scale, every variant's real Pearson sat below that gene-mean baseline (~0.847) — the correlation numbers on the previous slide were real, but they were mostly the trivial shortcut, not learned biological signal.
  • Scaling human-only 5k → 20k closed most (not all) of the gap: 0.69 → 0.81. Architecture tweaks alone, without more data, didn't move the needle nearly as much — scale was the dominant lever, not depth or regularization.
Building on the shared team pipeline (Walter Alvarado, Brian Zhou) — the diagnostic itself is mine.
06 · ~55s
My work, last semester

I built the team's first MoE gate — and the diagnostic that flagged it as premature

Idea: route between species-specialized experts (human / mouse / mixed) instead of using one monolithic model.

  • Built a training-free "oracle headroom" check (analyze_moe_headroom.py) that asks, before spending any compute on gate training: if you always picked the best of the three experts per sample, how much would you gain over just using the single best expert overall?
  • Also caught a bug with check_moe_gene_counts.py: each expert had trained on its own gene ordering, so the gate's inputs weren't even comparable across experts.

Near-zero headroom, on top of mismatched vocabularies and collapsed experts. My conclusion for the team: MoE was limited by expert quality, not routing — and that's the exact question this SPARC project picks up.

This project · SPARC continuation

Research question

"Can Mixture-of-Experts architectures improve predictive performance and biological interpretability for spaceflight transcriptomic classification — once the expert-quality problems that invalidated the first attempt are actually fixed?"

  • Objective 1 — implement and refine MoE architectures for transcriptomic classification, on top of experts that don't collapse and do share a vocabulary.
  • Objective 2 — evaluate expert specialization behavior across species, tissue, and stressor.
  • Objective 3 — benchmark against the existing baselines and assess biological interpretability, not just correlation numbers.
08 · ~40s
This sprint · The fix

v2: one shared gene vocabulary, deeper architecture

  • Built compute_shared_canonical.py — a canonical gene list (protein-coding orthologs ∩ human exon lengths ∩ mouse exon lengths) so human, mouse, and mixed experts all share identical column order. Directly fixes the vocab-mismatch bug from slide 7.
  • Moved to a deeper architecture (4 layers vs. 2, 30% mask ratio vs. 15%, weight decay 0.01) to give the model more room to escape gene-mean collapse, informed by the "scale is the real lever" finding from slide 6.
  • This sprint: rebuilding three v2 5k experts (human / mouse / mixed) under this fix, then re-running the same headroom diagnostic on data that's actually comparable this time.
